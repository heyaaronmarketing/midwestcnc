## Key Takeaways

- Standard bellows covers ship in 2 to 4 weeks for standard bellows from measurement approval.
- Telescoping steel covers run 4 to 8 weeks based on plate count and wiper setup.
- Incomplete measurements add 1 to 2 weeks to any project due to clarification cycles.
- OEM routing adds 2 to 4 weeks versus aftermarket fabrication.
- Legacy machines like pre-2005 Mori Seiki or Fadal models face the longest waits when OEM parts are gone.

A torn bellows or collapsed steel cover puts your spindle at risk. Chips and coolant reach the ways. The damage adds up fast. Every shop asks the same question: how long until the new cover arrives? The answer depends on cover style, measurement quality, and fabrication routing. For a Haas VF-2, a bellows might ship in 12 days. For a 1998 Fadal VMC, you may wait 10 weeks. This article breaks down the ranges and shows what speeds the process.

## Lead-Time Ranges by Cover Style

Cover style is the first driver of lead time. Each type needs different steps, materials, and checks.

Bellows covers are the fastest. They ship in 2 to 4 weeks for standard bellows. The fabric or vinyl cuts fast. Pleats form by heat or stitching. Frames use basic steel or aluminum. A simple X-axis bellows for a Haas VF-series might ship in 12 to 15 days. This assumes clean dimensions and stock material.

Roll-up covers fall in a similar range. Spring cassettes are often standard parts. The flexible material gets cut to length. Add a week if the roll-up has built-in wipers or deflectors.

Telescoping steel covers take the longest. These run 4 to 8 weeks. Plate count matters. A 7-plate Z-axis cover for an Okuma LB3000 takes longer than a 4-plate Y-axis cover for a Mazak VTC-200. Each plate needs laser cutting, forming, and wiper fitting. Assembly requires alignment so plates extend and retract under axis travel.

Hybrid or custom covers can stretch to 8 to 12 weeks. These combine steel sections with bellows transitions. They handle unusual 5-axis shapes. Engineering review and prototype cycles add time.

| Cover Style | Lead Time | Key Factors |
|-------------|-----------|-------------|
| Bellows | 2-4 weeks | Material type, frame complexity |
| Roll-up | 2-4 weeks | Cassette stock, built-in wipers |
| Telescoping steel | 4-8 weeks | Plate count, wiper type, scraper hardness |
| Hybrid or custom | 8-12 weeks | Engineering review, prototype cycles |

These ranges assume approved measurements on first submission. Back-and-forth extends any timeline.

## What Speeds Up the Process

Three factors cut lead times. Each is in your control or influence.

Complete measurements are the biggest help. When we get collapsed length, extended length, hole patterns, bolt sizes, and clear photos, work starts right away. We have seen projects ship in 10 days when every dimension is confirmed upfront. The [Way Covers Engineering](/insights/way-covers-engineering/) section covers what to measure.

Aftermarket fabrication often beats OEM channels. OEM parts route through dealers and warehouses. Each step adds handling time. Custom fabrication can cut 30 percent off OEM lead times. The fabricator works from your measurements without those steps.

Stock materials matter. Standard stainless sheet in 16, 18, or 20 gauge ships in 2 to 3 days from service centers. Specialty materials add time. High-temp fabric for bellows near chip conveyors may add a week. Hardened tool steel for heavy scrapers takes longer too. Specify standard materials when the application allows.

Fabricator trust also helps. A shop that sends clean, consistent packages earns priority when capacity is tight. That trust turns into schedule preference.

## What Slows It Down

Delays fall into four groups. All are common. All are avoidable.

Incomplete measurements add 1 to 2 weeks. Common gaps include missing collapsed length or unclear hole patterns. If the fabricator emails for answers, waits, and re-queues, that cycle alone adds 5 to 10 days.

OEM routing adds 2 to 4 weeks. The distribution model has benefits. Parts are tracked. Quality is controlled. But speed is not the strength. For [Okuma CNC Way Covers](/way-covers/okuma-cnc-way-covers/) or [Mazak CNC Way Covers](/way-covers/mazak-cnc-way-covers/) on current machines, the OEM can supply. The timeline reflects the chain.

Legacy machines create the most variable delays. When a machine is out of production, the OEM may not stock covers. [Mori Seiki CNC Way Covers](/way-covers/mori-seiki-cnc-way-covers/) for pre-merger machines (before 2013) often need custom builds. Original part numbers are gone. [Fadal CNC Way Covers](/way-covers/fadal-cnc-way-covers/) for 1990s VMCs face similar gaps. The company changed hands. Parts supply is uneven. Custom fabrication from field measurements may be the only path. Engineering review for a first-time build adds 1 to 3 weeks before cutting starts.

Fabricator capacity affects all projects. Cover shops are small. A 3-week backlog can stretch a 2-week bellows job to 5 weeks. Ask about current capacity before committing.

## OEM vs. Custom Routing

The choice depends on machine age, timeline, and cost.

Current machines (roughly 2015 and newer) usually have OEM parts. If the machine is under contract, OEM parts may be required. Even without that, OEM parts offer exact fit. The tradeoff is time. OEM routing adds 2 to 4 weeks.

Legacy machines often have no OEM option. For [Makino CNC Way Covers](/way-covers/makino-cnc-way-covers/) on 1990s horizontals or [Haas CNC Way Covers](/way-covers/haas-cnc-way-covers/) on 20-year-old VF machines, original covers may be gone. Scarcity premiums apply if they exist at all. Custom fabrication becomes the practical path.

Timeline pressure favors custom routing. A collapsed cover exposes ways and ballscrews. Every day adds risk. Spindle damage from way contamination can cost $8,000 to $25,000. A 2-week faster delivery justifies the custom route.

Cost varies by situation. OEM covers for common machines are sometimes priced well. Volume drives efficiency. Custom covers need engineering time. But for discontinued machines, scarcity pricing can make custom work 20 to 40 percent cheaper.

## Rush Orders and Premiums

When downtime costs exceed premiums, rush orders are an option.

Rush work can compress bellows from 2-4 weeks to 7-10 days. Steel covers can move from 4-8 weeks to 3-4 weeks. The compression has limits. Material, cutting, and forming still take time.

Premium charges run 25 to 50 percent above standard pricing. The premium covers overtime, priority sourcing, and schedule disruption.

Shipping adds variables. Ground freight across the region adds 2 to 4 days. Air freight delivers next-day but costs 3 to 5 times more. For a $1,200 cover, $300 in air freight may make sense. For a $6,000 steel assembly, the math changes.

The decision comes down to risk. A failed cover allows chips onto precision surfaces. Daily damage risk can exceed the rush premium in days.

## Measurement Quality: The Variable You Control

Of all factors, measurement quality is the one you control. Poor measurements are the most common delay source. They are also avoidable.

A complete package includes:

- Collapsed length (axis at minimum travel)
- Extended length (axis at maximum travel)
- Width and height of the cover envelope
- Hole patterns with bolt sizes and spacing
- Wiper groove dimensions if replacing wipers
- Photos of the cover, brackets, and damage
- Machine make, model, serial, and axis

Clean measurements let work start without questions. We have seen 10-day bellows jobs with good data. The same cover takes 25 days with three clarification rounds.

For steel covers, collapsed and extended measurements are critical. Plate overlap determines if the cover seals. A quarter-inch error in collapsed length can cause binding at full extension. It can leave gaps at mid-travel.
