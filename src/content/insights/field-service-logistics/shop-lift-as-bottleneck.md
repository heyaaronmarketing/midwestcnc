## Key Takeaways

- Most vertical machining centers weigh between 8,000 and 25,000 pounds, requiring overhead crane capacity of at least 5 tons for safe handling during motor swaps, spindle pulls, or column work.
- A 2-ton jib crane covers spindle and axis motor work on machines under 12,000 pounds, but anything heavier demands bridge crane access or external rigging.
- When your shop's lift capacity falls short, the spindle ships out to a rebuild facility rather than waiting weeks for rigging availability.
- The four shops Ken has worked around in the last decade each solved lift limitations differently—jib cranes, shared bridge cranes, rigging contracts, or shipping components instead of machines.
- Forklift-only shops handling lathes or horizontal mills above 10,000 pounds typically add 2-4 days to any service call requiring component removal.

A 15,000-pound VMC needs to come out of production for a spindle rebuild. Your forklift is rated to 6,000 pounds. The machine sits 40 feet from the overhead crane that belongs to the tenant next door. This happens in Midwest shops more often than anyone admits. Lift capacity determines whether a repair takes three days or three weeks. It shapes routing decisions for field service. It dictates whether a spindle ships out or gets pulled on-site. Sometimes it kills job quotes entirely when a shop cannot safely move its own equipment.

## Adequate Lift Capacity Starts with Component Weights

The baseline question is simple. Can you get the heaviest component off the machine and onto a pallet without improvising? For most CNC repair scenarios, that component is the spindle cartridge, the spindle motor, or a servo drive assembly. Spindle cartridges on mid-size VMCs typically weigh 150-400 pounds. Spindle motors range from 80 to 250 pounds. These weights work with a chain hoist, an engine crane, or a small jib.

The problem arises when the repair requires separating the head from the column. Dropping a ballscrew assembly creates similar challenges. Lifting the entire spindle housing as a unit demands more capacity. On a Mazak VTC-300C, the spindle head assembly weighs over 1,200 pounds. On a Doosan DNM 650, the spindle cartridge plus motor plus housing approaches 900 pounds. A 2-ton jib crane handles these pulls comfortably. A forklift with a boom attachment does not. The geometry is wrong. The reach is limited. The risk of dropping a $40,000 spindle onto concrete is real.

For [CNC Machine Repair Services](/repairs/) that involve column-mounted heads, the safe threshold is a 5-ton overhead crane. This covers the full range of 40-taper and 50-taper VMCs from Haas, Mazak, DMG Mori, Doosan, and Okuma. Horizontal machining centers and larger lathes push into 10-ton territory. A Mazak Integrex e-500H weighs over 50,000 pounds as a complete unit. Even component work on that platform assumes heavy rigging infrastructure.

## How Four Different Shops Solved Lift Limitations

Ken has worked around lift constraints at dozens of facilities over three decades. Four shops illustrate the common patterns.

**Shop A in Cedar Rapids** runs a job shop with 14 VMCs in a leased building. The lease prohibits overhead crane installation. Their solution: a rigging company on retainer. Whenever a spindle pull exceeds their 3-ton portable gantry capacity, they schedule the riggers 48 hours out. This adds cost. Figure $800-$1,500 per lift event. But it preserves their ability to handle [spindle rebuild and grinding](/spindle-grinding/) turnaround on-site.

**Shop B outside Rockford** shares a building with a metal fabrication outfit. The fab shop owns a 10-ton bridge crane spanning both bays. Shop B negotiated crane access as part of their sublease. When Ken was there for a [Fanuc Series 30i / 31i / 32i](/repairs/fanuc-cnc-machine-repair/series-30i-31i-32i/) control swap requiring head removal, the bridge crane cut a two-day rigging delay down to 45 minutes.

**Shop C in the Des Moines metro** installed a 2-ton jib crane for maintenance work. This came after losing a spindle motor to a forklift mishap. The jib covers their Haas VF-4 and VF-6 fleet, all under 18,000 pounds. For their one Okuma LB3000, which needs periodic tailstock work at component weights around 600 pounds, the jib reaches with six inches to spare.

**Shop D near Omaha** chose a different path entirely. Their building has 16-foot ceilings and no crane infrastructure. Rather than retrofit, they ship spindles out for every rebuild. When a [Spindle Service on Haas Next Generation Control (NGC)](/spindle-grinding/haas-spindle-repair/haas-ngc/) machine needs bearing work, the spindle ships out to our bench in Waterloo. This adds 2-3 days of transit time. But it avoids crane installation capital and rigging coordination.

## When the Spindle Ships Out Instead

The decision tree for on-site versus ship-out depends on three variables. Machine weight matters. Available lift capacity matters. Turnaround pressure matters.

If your forklift is rated to 6,000 pounds and your largest machine weighs 12,000 pounds, you cannot pull spindles on-site without additional rigging. The math fails. In these cases, the spindle ships out. We see this pattern regularly in [Multi-State Spindle Logistics: Shipping from Texas](/insights/field-service-logistics/multi-state-spindle-logistics/). Shops 500+ miles from our bench make the calculation that crating and freight beats scheduling local rigging and an on-site tech for the exact hours.

The breakeven point varies by urgency. A shop with a production deadline in 72 hours cannot wait for rigging availability next Tuesday. The spindle ships out overnight. It hits our bench the next morning. It returns rebuilt within the week. A shop with more schedule flexibility might wait for rigging. Ken pulls the spindle on-site. Freight costs disappear entirely.

What we have seen across our [Field Service & Logistics (Midwest)](/insights/field-service-logistics/) territory is that shops without dedicated overhead cranes default to ship-out workflows. This applies to anything beyond basic motor swaps. This is not a failure. It is a rational response to infrastructure constraints. Shipping a 300-pound spindle cartridge costs $200-$400 each way. Renting a crane and rigger for a single pull costs $1,200-$2,000 plus scheduling delays. The economics favor shipping unless you pull spindles often enough to justify fixed lift infrastructure.

## Minimum Crane Specs by Machine Class

For shops evaluating crane installation or upgrade, here are the capacity thresholds we typically see as sufficient.

**Benchtop and small VMCs under 6,000 pounds:** A 1-ton chain hoist or jib crane handles all component work. Engine cranes work in a pinch. These machines rarely require lifting anything heavier than a spindle motor at 100-150 pounds.

**Mid-size VMCs from 6,000 to 18,000 pounds:** A 2-ton jib crane with 10-foot reach covers spindle pulls, head work, and ballscrew drops on most 40-taper machines. This is the sweet spot for single-machine shops. Small job shops running Haas VF series, Doosan DNM series, or similar platforms fit here.

**Large VMCs and small HMCs from 18,000 to 35,000 pounds:** Minimum 5-ton bridge crane or gantry. Jib cranes at this capacity exist but require substantial column foundations. Most shops at this machine size share building crane access or maintain rigging contracts.

**Large HMCs, multi-axis machines, and heavy lathes above 35,000 pounds:** 10-ton or higher bridge crane. At this scale, the crane is typically a building feature. It is not a shop addition. Shops without it plan around rigging company availability for any major service event.

For [replacement way covers](/way-covers/) work, lift requirements are lighter. Way cover assemblies rarely exceed 200 pounds even on large machines. The constraint there is access and workspace, not lift capacity.

## Coordinating Lift and Tech Schedules

The logistical challenge is not just having a crane. It is having the crane available when the technician arrives. This coordination problem drives much of the planning in [Coordinating a Multi-Day On-Site Service Trip](/insights/field-service-logistics/multi-day-onsite-coordination/).

A typical multi-day on-site job might require crane access on day one for disassembly. No crane on days two and three during bench work or parts-waiting. Crane access again on day four for reassembly. If your crane is shared with other tenants or requires a rigging company callout, those day-one and day-four windows need to be locked before the tech books travel.

We have seen jobs slip a full week because of timing conflicts. The rigging company was available Monday. The tech was booked through Wednesday. The shop floor had a production run blocking crane access Friday. The fix is sequencing the booking. Confirm rigging availability first. Then schedule the tech. Then communicate the production-floor hold to operations. Getting this wrong once teaches most shops to plan lift access as the first constraint. Not an afterthought.

## The Real Cost of Undersized Lift Capacity

Shops that chronically work around lift limitations pay in three ways.

**Extended downtime.** A spindle pull that takes two hours with adequate crane access takes two days when waiting for rigging availability. Multiply that across four or five service events per year. The production cost dwarfs any crane investment.

**Risk of damage.** Improvised lifting creates problems. Forklifts at odd angles. Chain hoists rigged to ceiling joists not rated for point loads. Two guys with a hydraulic jack. These approaches damage spindles. They drop motors. They occasionally injure people. We have rebuilt spindles that arrived with fresh impact damage from lift mishaps.

**Deferred maintenance.** When every spindle service requires a rigging event, shops defer maintenance longer than they should. Bearings run past their service window. Vibration issues compound. By the time the shop schedules the rigging and the pull, what could have been a bearing swap has become a full spindle rebuild.

Installing a 2-ton jib crane in an existing building typically runs $8,000-$15,000. Cost depends on column foundation requirements and electrical integration. That investment pays back quickly if you currently spend $1,500 per rigging callout. Average three callouts per year and the math works out within 24 months.
